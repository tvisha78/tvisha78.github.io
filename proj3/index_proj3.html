Part 3A: Image Warping and Mosaicing

This part goes into the cool application of image mosaicing. We'll take two or more photographs and create an image mosaic by registering, projective warping, resampling, and compositing them. Along the way, we'll learn to compute homographies, and to use them to warp images.

Part A.1: Capture and digitize pictures

To find correspondences between images, it is important to ensure that the images are related by a projective/perspective transformation. One common way to achieve this is by fixing the center of projection (COP) and rotating the camera while capturing the photos. This approach ensures that all images contain the same information about the light rays, which is crucial when stitching them together. The diagram below illustrates why keeping the same COP is important for accurate image stitching.

Part A.2: Recover Homographies

Before we can warp the images into alignment, we need to recover the parameters of the transformation between each pair. As described below, the transformation is a homography: p'=Hp, where H is a 3x3 matrix with 8 degrees of freedom (the lower right is a scaling factor is is set to 1). \[ H = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] The homography transformation is defined as \[ p' = Hp \] \[ \begin{bmatrix} p_x' \\ p_y' \\ w' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} p_x \\ p_y \\ w \end{bmatrix} \] In order to find the parameters of homography matrix H, we need to
  1. Find corresponding points between two images
    • Since this is a homography matrix and we have 8 unknowns, we need at least 4 corresponding points (each point gives two equations for the x and y). However, to make our solution more robust to noise, we will use more than 4 points and build a overconstrained system
    • In this section, we are MANUALLY establishing the point correspondences using a mouse-clicking interface
  2. Set up a linear system of n equations
    • We can solve the overdetermined system using least squares
  3. Solve the linear system of equations and compute H = computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts)
    • im1_pts and im2_pts are nx2 matrices of the corresponding points
We can set up the system of equations using the correspondences. Here the w is simply the scaling factor, so x' and y' are divided by w to get back the coordinates in 2D. Multiplying the matrices out, we can get \[ \frac{p_x'}{w'} = \frac{a p_x + b p_y + c}{g p_x + h p_y + 1} \quad{and}\quad \frac{p_y'}{w'} = \frac{d p_x + e p_y + f}{g p_x + h p_y + 1} \] We can isolate the unknowns and arrange the least squares equation for one point as \[ Ah = b \] \[ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -xx' & -yx' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 & -xy' & -yy' \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1'\\ y_1' \end{bmatrix} \] To find the correspondences, I tried to choose edges that would be easy to pick between the two images. The correspondances are color coordinated.

Correspondances Visualized

After solving the least squares, the recovered homography matrix for the images above was \[ H = \begin{bmatrix} 2.12114409 & 0.03477176 & -594.870436 \\ 0.380436377 & 1.72216329 & -171.641533 \\ 0.001332330 & 1.6372e{-7} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Comparison: Nearest Neighbor vs. Bilinear Interpolation

  • Nearest Neighbor interpolation is computationally simpler and faster, but it often produces visible artifacts such as blocky textures and jagged edges, especially along diagonals or when the image is scaled significantly.
  • Bilinear interpolation is slightly slower since it averages values from four neighbors, but it produces smoother transitions and more visually natural results, making it better suited for image warping and stitching tasks.
Implementation details:
  • When we warp with a homography, the output image may not have the same size as the input. If we just assume that the rectified image has the same shape as the original image, parts of the warped image will get cut out. Instead, we can predict the output size to avoid cropping:
    • Take the corners of the input image.
    • Apply the homography to these corners to get their coordinates in the warped space.
    • Find the bounding box that covers the transformed corners.
  • We can apply an alpha channel to handle undefined pixels. If we only have the RGB channels, undefined pixels are filled with black by default, which is misleading because we cannot tell whether black pixels are real content or empty background. By adding an alpha channel, we explicitly store opacity and clearly separate content from background.

To stitch the images, I first computed correspondences and used them to estimate a homography transformation (H matrix). Since warping with a homography changes the geometry, the warped image may extend beyond the original size and even into negative coordinates. To handle this, I transformed the input image's corners with H, found the bounding box of those transformed corners, and used it to predict the correct output size. I applied an offset based on the bounding box minimums to shift everything into non-negative coordinates, ensuring that both the warped image and the reference image could be aligned and placed properly on the same canvas without cropping.

When overlapping two images, edge artifacts often appear along the seam. To blend them seamlessly, I use weighted averaging (per-pixel blending) with distance-based weights. I first compute the distance transform for each image's alpha mask: this gives, for every pixel, its distance to the nearest background (empty) pixel. Intuitively, pixels that are farther from edges should contribute more to the blend, while pixels near edges should contribute less. In the overlapping region, I normalize these distances to obtain weights (so the two weights sum to 1 at each pixel), then apply a per-pixel weighted average of the RGB values from both images. Outside the overlap, each image is kept fully opaque. This gives blended mosaic. Let's walk through this process for one of the mosaic.

Part 3B: Feature Matching for Autostitching

The goal of this section is to create a system for automatically stitching images into a mosaic. A secondary goal is to learn how to read and implement a research paper.

B.1: Harris Corner Detection

We can use Harris corners to detect features in an image. The idea behind the Harris corner detector is that we want to easily recognize a point through a small window, such that shifting this window in any direction causes a large change in intensity.

  • If the change occurs only in one direction (x or y), it indicates an edge.
  • If there is little to no change in any direction, it indicates a flat region.
  • If the intensity changes significantly in both directions, it indicates a corner.
The Harris Corner Detector identifies locations with strong intensity variation in both x and y directions: these points are considered corners that are robust to translation and rotation. The measure of corner response represents how strong or corner-like a point is in the image. It is defined as: \[ R = \frac{\det(M)}{\text{trace}(M)} \] where M is the second moment matrix at each pixel: \[ M = \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_xI_y \\ I_xI_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

However, as described in the Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches paper, one desirable quality is to select a subset of corners that have both a strong corner response and are spatially well distributed across the image. This is particularly useful for panorama applications, as the overlap between images could be small and thus we want well distributed feature points. To achieve this, we implement Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS), where for each corner we find the minimum distance to the next stronger corner. Points that are very close to stronger ones get suppressed (small radius), points that are far from any stronger points are kept (large radius), and finally we select the top N corners with the largest suppression radii. This ensures that the resulting keypoints are both strong and evenly distributed across the image. Here I kept the 500 points for ANMS.

B.2: Feature Descriptor Extraction

Now that we determined our interest points using Harris corners and ANMS, we need a way to extract the description around that corner, so that we can realiably match corners across overlapping images. A feature descriptor encodes how the image looks around a corner into a numerical vector. By comparing these descriptors between images, we can tell which corners represent the same real-world point despite changes in viewpoint or lighting. Based on the MOPS paper, the general steps to implement the feature descriptors are oulined below.
  1. Apply Gaussian to the image before sampling
    This makes the patches less sensitive to the exact feature location. Intuitively, we sample pixels at a lower frequency than where the interest points are detected, which stabilizes the descriptor
  2. Extract a 40x40 window centered at each corner
  3. Subsample every 5 pixels to obtain an 8x8 patch
    To avoid aliasing, perform the sampling at a higher pyramid level (i.e., a more blurred image, so high-frequency details are already smoothed out) such that the effective sampling rate is approximately once per pixel
  4. Normalize the descriptor to remove bias/gain
    Bias/gain normalization makes the features invariant to affine changes in intensity (overall brightness and contrast)

B.3: Feature Matching

We can now use the feature descriptors to find pairs of features that look similar and are likely to be correspondences between images. To do this, I implemented the method described in the MOPS paper, performing feature matching with Lowe's ratio to select pairs that are most likely correct matches.

  • Nearest neighbors: For each descriptor in Image 1, find the two nearest neighbors in Image 2 based on the Euclidean distance.
  • Lowe's ratio test: Compute the ratio r = d1 / d2, where d1 is the distance to the closest match and d2 is the distance to the second-closest match. Accept the match only if r is less than the threshold (i.e., the best match is significantly better than the second-best).
  • Threshold: I used a threshold of 0.4 (looking at Figure 6b in the MOPS paper). A lower threshold means there is a larger difference between the best and second-best distances.

This procedure favors matches where the best candidate is much better than the next alternative, reducing outliers and improving the reliability and accuracy of the overall matching.

B.4: RANSAC for Robust Homography

I used the 4-point RANSAC algorithm to compute a robust homography between image pairs. This approach makes the homography estimation resistant to incorrect matches and outliers, ensuring that the final transformation is based only on reliable feature correspondences.

RANSAC Loop

  1. Select four feature pairs at random
  2. Compute a candidate homography H from those 4 correspondences
  3. Classify inliers: Count all matches satisfying dist(p', H p) < error
  4. Track the model with the largest inlier set across iterations.
  5. Recompute a least-squares homography using all inliers from the best iteration.

I used the same blending techniques from Part A (e.g., alpha masks / weighted averaging) to seamlessly merge overlapping regions after warping with the estimated homography.
